Is it safe to upload your photo and ID to an online resizer?

By Shivendra Kumar Sonkar · Published 2026-11-02

Exam photos, signatures, Aadhaar-linked forms and certificates are personal data. Yet people routinely drop them onto the first "free online resizer" a search shows. Whether that is safe depends entirely on one question: does the tool send your file to a server?

Two kinds of online tools

  • Upload-based tools send your file to the company's server, process it there and send it back. Your photo sits on someone else's machine for some period — often with a policy you have not read. Some delete files within hours; others keep them, use them for analytics or training, or simply store them for longer than they say.
  • Browser-based tools download a program into your browser and process the file locally using your own device. The file is never transmitted anywhere, so there is nothing to store or leak.

How to check for yourself

You do not have to take any site's word for it. In Chrome, Edge or Firefox, press F12, open the Network tab, then process a test file. If the tool is browser-based you will see page assets loading but no request that carries your image or PDF. If you see a large "POST" or "upload" request the size of your file, it is being sent away. Disconnecting from the internet after the page has loaded is another easy test: a genuinely local tool keeps working.

What SnapFit does

Being specific matters more than a blanket promise, so here is what SnapFit actually sends:

  • Resizing, cropping, compression and PDF compression run in your browser using the canvas and PDF libraries. Your photo or PDF is not uploaded.
  • Background removal uses a machine-learning model that is downloaded to your browser and runs locally on your photo.
  • QR codes are drawn in your browser; the link, text and logo you enter are not sent anywhere.
  • The optional "read my notification" feature is the one exception. If you choose to upload a screenshot of an exam notice so SnapFit can read the size requirements, that screenshot is sent to a server-side reading service. Your photo is not part of that. You only use this feature if you click it.
  • Analytics and advertising. Like most free sites, SnapFit uses basic analytics and may show Google ads, which can set cookies. This concerns how the page is used, not the contents of your files. The privacy policy explains the details and how to opt out of personalised ads.

Habits that protect you on any site

  1. Prefer tools that say plainly where processing happens, and check it with the Network tab.
  2. Never upload a document that shows more than the task needs. If a form wants only your photo, do not upload a page that also shows your Aadhaar number.
  3. Be wary of tools that demand an account just to resize one image.
  4. Read the privacy policy for a retention period and a contact address. A missing contact is a red flag.
  5. Delete downloaded copies from shared computers, such as a cyber café's, when you are done.

The bottom line

For personal photos and identity documents, a browser-based tool is the safer default because there is no copy to go astray. SnapFit is built that way, and the owner, contact details and privacy policy are published so you can reach a real person if something looks wrong.
